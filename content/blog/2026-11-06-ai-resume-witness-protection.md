I have more than twenty years of technology experience. Apparently, that was too much identifying information.

While helping me apply for jobs, an AI assistant produced a two-page résumé that removed my name, my professional email, my company, my university, and the names of the organizations where I had worked.

It left the degree. Somewhere in America, a Bachelor of Arts had happened.

My career had entered witness protection.

Instead of InXpress, the document offered “Global shipping and logistics platform.” SeeTickets became “Event-ticketing platform.” Intermountain became “Regional healthcare system.”

I had spent years solving real problems for real organizations. The résumé presented me as a technical architect who had done some things in several buildings.

Some of the buildings were regional.

Then a rejection email arrived, and I asked which résumé we had submitted.

The answer was the anonymous one.

Of course that hurt my chances.

A hiring team was trying to decide whether I could do a specific job. We had removed information that would help them make that decision. Company names, recognizable engagements, a verifiable professional identity, the context behind the work: gone.

We had made the evidence harder to evaluate and given a busy reader more work.

What an excellent competitive strategy. Perhaps next time I could submit a blank PDF and let them enjoy the mystery.

The explanation was privacy.

I do want my home address, private phone number, credentials and other sensitive details kept out of a public résumé. My professional name and the companies in my professional history serve a rather different purpose.

The system had stretched a useful instruction until it swallowed the purpose of the document.

“Protect my personal information” had become “ensure nobody can establish who performed this work.”

That is an impressive security boundary. It also works against employment.

There was a second helpful optimization: get everything down to two pages.

I have worked across healthcare, logistics, ticketing, finance, energy, mobile applications, backend systems, cloud infrastructure and developer tooling. I run Nokware. The work overlaps because consulting engagements often do.

That history requires judgment to explain clearly.

A page limit is much easier.

So the workflow compressed the career into roughly six hundred words. A great deal of evidence disappeared. The document remained tidy.

My résumé had excellent margins. Its employment prospects were left to fend for themselves.

When I checked the rejection notices against the saved submissions, the damage was visible. Several applications had gone out with anonymized versions. Others had retained my identity but used heavily shortened histories. One company had sent a rejection that did not even identify which of three applications it concerned; all three had received anonymized résumés.

We had successfully automated the distribution of weaker evidence.

The PDFs opened. The layouts looked professional. Reviews had passed. Applications had been submitted. Confirmation pages existed.

Every little light on the dashboard could be green while the actual business outcome was being undermined.

This is a software problem I recognize immediately when it happens to somebody else.

An API returns success. A queue drains. A file arrives. A deployment completes.

Then you ask whether the customer can actually do the thing they came to do.

In this case, the “customer” was me, and the thing was getting a hiring team to understand why twenty-plus years of work might be useful to them.

We had verified that the attachment arrived.

We needed to verify that the attachment still represented the applicant.

That distinction now has concrete checks behind it.

The résumé starts from my complete career history. Tailoring emphasizes the work relevant to the role and keeps the evidence attached to the engagement where it happened. My professional identity, company, actual employers and clients, university, dates and meaningful accomplishments remain intact.

Private contact details stay private where appropriate.

Before submission, the exact final PDF gets checked for its content as well as its appearance. The résumé associated with the actual application gets verified too. A filename sitting in an editor is not proof that the employer received that attachment.

And an instruction to make something concise no longer has permission to silently remove the reason it matters.

This lesson extends well beyond résumés.

A proposal can lose its strongest case study. A sales email can become so polished that it stops saying anything. A support reply can satisfy a template while ignoring the customer’s problem. An agent can complete every step in a workflow and still make the business worse at what it was trying to accomplish.

The failure becomes easier to scale precisely because the outputs look respectable.

I still use AI. There is plenty of useful work for it to do.

But the acceptance criteria now include the inconvenient question: did this preserve the evidence and accomplish the purpose, or did it merely produce something that looks finished?

That question would have been cheaper to ask before the applications went out.

My name is Mish Ochu. I run Nokware.

I am putting both of those facts back on the résumé.

The witness protection program was terrible for networking.
