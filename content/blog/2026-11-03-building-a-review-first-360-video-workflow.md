I wanted the flexibility of a Resolve project with several timelines: a landscape video, vertical Shorts, and Instagram versions from the same source material. Before that could replace another editing step, the stitching had to be convincing.

The source material came from an October 21 outing. The processing and validation described here happened on November 3. Keeping those dates separate helps distinguish the recorded event from the engineering work performed on it.

### Start with a proof small enough to inspect

The first useful deliverable was a four-second proof with editable subject-tracking keyframes and separate output timelines. It answered whether the proposed path could produce something worth continuing.

Lens guards turned out to be relevant. The footage used premium glass guards, while the starting calibration was for bare lenses. Applying the appropriate calibration substantially improved the stitching.

A short success still left important questions open: longer motion, difficult seams, tilt, recognition, and final-resolution quality. The next step was a 30-second review rather than declaring the workflow complete.

### Review the whole source before polishing the whole source

The work expanded to ten chronologically ordered clips, totaling about 70 minutes with audio. Lower-resolution review copies made that material practical to browse. A local 360 player let me inspect the scene and identify a time and viewing direction for a later edit.

That changes the editorial conversation. A request can describe a specific moment and target, then become editable pan, tilt, zoom, and transition keyframes. The review artifact helps establish what the edit should do before committing to expensive final renders.

### Keep the distinction between review and delivery

The resulting review copies still had residual seam and tilt issues. The original footage remained available, and the projects and review exports were verified on the Mac as well.

At this stage, the evidence supported a usable review workflow and editable timelines. It did not establish that the original stitching application could be retired for every shot.

The broader lesson is about feedback speed. A small visual proof, a longer motion test, and a browsable source review answer different questions. Keeping those stages explicit makes it easier to find a defect before it has been multiplied across every output format.
